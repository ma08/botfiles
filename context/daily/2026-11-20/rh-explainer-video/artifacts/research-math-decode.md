## 1. Background

### 1.1 What RH says [BG]

**Primes and the counting function.** π(x) is the number of primes up to x. Gauss guessed that the density of primes near t is about 1/log t, which gives the approximation Li(x) = ∫₂ˣ dt/log t. The Prime Number Theorem (Hadamard and de la Vallée Poussin, 1896) says π(x)/Li(x) → 1.

**The error term.** RH is equivalent to the statement that the error is about as small as it can be:

> |π(x) − Li(x)| ≤ C · √x · log x.

Schoenfeld (1976) made this explicit: under RH, |π(x) − li(x)| < √x log x / (8π) for x ≥ 2657. Without RH, the best proven error terms are much weaker (they don't even reach x^0.999).

**Zeta.** For Re s > 1,

> ζ(s) = Σ n^(−s) = Π_p (1 − p^(−s))^(−1).

The product over primes (Euler) is why ζ knows about primes. Riemann (1859) showed ζ extends to the whole complex plane except for a simple pole at s = 1 (analytic continuation), and satisfies a functional equation relating s and 1 − s.

**Zeros.** ζ vanishes at −2, −4, −6, ... (the trivial zeros). All other zeros lie in the critical strip 0 < Re s < 1, symmetric about the line Re s = 1/2. **RH: every nontrivial zero has real part exactly 1/2.** The first is at about 1/2 + 14.1347i. More than 10^13 zeros have been verified numerically to lie on the line (Gourdon 2004; Platt and Trudgian 2021 rigorously to height 3·10^12).

**The explicit formula.** The cleanest prime-counting function is Chebyshev's ψ(x) = Σ_{n≤x} Λ(n), where the von Mangoldt function Λ(n) equals log p if n is a power of the prime p and 0 otherwise. Then

> ψ(x) = x − Σ_ρ x^ρ/ρ − log 2π − ½ log(1 − x^(−2)),

the sum running over nontrivial zeros ρ. Each zero ρ = β + iγ contributes an oscillation of size x^β and "frequency" γ in log x. This is the "music of the primes": the zeros are the frequencies, the real parts are the loudness. If all β = 1/2, every wave has amplitude √x and ψ(x) = x + O(√x log² x). A single zero with β > 1/2 would produce a louder wave and a bigger error.

**Weil's positivity criterion.** Weil (1952) restated RH as a positivity statement: a certain functional W built from the explicit formula (the Guinand-Weil explicit formula) is nonnegative on all test functions of a specific "f convolved with its reflection" shape if and only if RH holds. This is relevant because the note works with "W(·) ≥ 0".

### 1.2 What GRH adds [BG]

A Dirichlet character χ mod q is a periodic multiplicative function on the integers. Its L-function L(s, χ) = Σ χ(n) n^(−s) governs primes in arithmetic progressions a mod q. **GRH (most common usage): every zero of every Dirichlet L-function in the critical strip lies on Re s = 1/2.**

Why it matters:

- **Primes in progressions.** Under GRH, π(x; q, a) = Li(x)/φ(q) + O(√x log x), uniformly in q. Unconditionally we only have this for tiny q (Siegel-Walfisz) or on average (Bombieri-Vinogradov).
- **Primality testing.** Under GRH, the least witness in Miller's test is at most 2(log n)² (Bach), so Miller-Rabin becomes a fast deterministic test. Note: AKS (2002) already gives unconditional deterministic polynomial-time primality testing, just slower. GRH has no direct bearing on breaking encryption.
- **Small generators and non-residues.** Under GRH the least quadratic non-residue mod p is O(log² p); unconditionally the bound is a small power of p.
- **Class numbers and algebraic number theory.** GRH rules out Siegel zeros, giving effective lower bounds for class numbers of imaginary quadratic fields. It gives Bach's bound (class group generated by primes of norm ≤ 12 log²|D|), which makes class group computations provably fast. Effective Chebotarev (Lagarias-Odlyzko) and Hooley's conditional proof of Artin's primitive root conjecture use the hypothesis for Dedekind zeta functions.
- Hundreds of theorems are stated "assuming GRH".

### 1.3 "Extended RH" [BG + flagged]

Terminology is not uniform in the literature.

- Convention A (most common): GRH = Dirichlet L-functions. ERH = Dedekind zeta functions of number fields (and often Hecke L-functions).
- Convention B: the two names swapped.
- "Grand RH": all automorphic L-functions.

**[NOTE]** The note defines neither term. It claims RH, says the method "easily extends" to GRH, and says the author *expects* ERH "with more time". So ERH is **not** claimed. In his usage ERH is something beyond GRH, which fits Convention A, but that's [READ]. The statement in the Lean archive is what decides what "GRH" means here. The video should say "RH and a generalization called GRH" and not define ERH precisely.

## 2. The function-field story

### 2.1 Weil, Stepanov, Bombieri, Schmidt, Deligne [BG]

**The analogy.** Replace the integers Z by polynomials F_q[t] over a finite field. Primes become irreducible polynomials; more generally, a curve C over F_q has a zeta function that counts its points over F_q, F_{q²}, F_{q³}, ... This zeta function is a rational function of q^(−s), with 2g "zeros" (g = genus).

**Weil's theorem (1940s).** All those zeros have |α| = √q, which is RH for the curve. Equivalent form: |#C(F_q) − (q + 1)| ≤ 2g√q. Hasse had done elliptic curves in the 1930s. Weil's proofs used intersection theory on the surface C × C or the Jacobian.

**Deligne (1974).** The same for varieties of any dimension (the last Weil conjecture), using étale cohomology. One ingredient is a positivity trick with even tensor powers going back to Rankin: you learn about the size of eigenvalues by studying high even powers, where a sign is known. Keep this in mind for the note's "M^k".

**Stepanov (1969).** An elementary proof for certain curves (for example y² = f(x)), with no algebraic geometry. Bombieri (1973) gave a short version for all curves using Riemann-Roch. Schmidt (1970s) gave elementary treatments of further cases.

**How Stepanov's method works.** Take y² = f(x) over F_p. You want to count x in F_p where f(x) is a square.

1. Build an auxiliary polynomial r(x), not identically zero, that vanishes **to order at least m** at every such x.
2. Keep its degree D under control.
3. A nonzero polynomial of degree D has at most D zeros counted with multiplicity. So (number of points) × m ≤ D, hence (number of points) ≤ D/m.

The construction uses two facts special to characteristic p: x^p = x on F_p, and the derivative of x^p is zero. So one can write r(x) as a combination of terms a_j(x)·x^(pj), differentiate as though the x^(pj) were constants, and then substitute x^p = x. That gives many vanishing conditions cheaply. (Hasse derivatives are used instead of ordinary ones because ordinary derivatives of order ≥ p vanish identically in characteristic p.) With m chosen around √p, the bound becomes p + O(√p). The lower bound comes from applying the same argument to the complementary set.

**Why multiplicity is the crux.** With m = 1 the argument gives nothing: "a polynomial vanishing at N points has degree at least N" is trivial, and the auxiliary polynomial costs more degree than it saves. The square-root saving appears only because each point is charged m times against the degree budget, and the vanishing conditions come at a discount. And multiplicity requires differentiation.

**The extension-field step.** Bombieri's version proves the upper bound N_n ≤ q^n + 1 + C·q^(n/2) for fields F_{q^n} with n large. A matching lower bound is obtained first (by a covering trick; an upper bound alone isn't enough). To get from the two-sided bound to |α_i| = √q exactly, you use all n at once: a pigeonhole (Dirichlet approximation) argument finds n where all the α_i^n nearly point the same way, so no cancellation hides a large one. This is the "pigeonhole on F_{q^n}/F_q" in item 11.

### 2.2 Why this is hard over the integers [BG]

On polynomials F_q[t] there is a derivative d/dt. On the integers there is none: any derivation on Z is zero, because d(1) = 0 forces d(n) = 0. "Seven" has no variable to differentiate with respect to. So the object that powers Stepanov's proof has no direct counterpart for the Riemann zeta function.

**[NOTE]** The author says exactly this: multiplicity is hard to find over the integers, "corresponding to something like differentiating whole numbers."

**The F1 dream.** A long-standing hope is that Z behaves like a curve over a mythical "field with one element" F1. Then Spec Z × Spec Z (over F1) would be a surface on which Weil's intersection-theory proof could be rerun. Nobody has built this.

**Borger's λ-rings.** Borger proposed that the extra structure of a λ-ring (a commuting family of lifts of Frobenius, one for each prime) should be regarded as descent data from Z to F1. A lift of Frobenius ψ_p gives a "p-derivation" δ(x) = (ψ_p(x) − x^p)/p; on Z itself this is the Fermat quotient (n − n^p)/p. This is one serious answer to "what is the derivative of a whole number".

**[NOTE]** fn 5 mentions thinking about how hard "fighting for some sort of F1 à la e.g. Borger would be". **[READ]** I take this to mean the author considered the F1 route and did not go that way. The theta-lift chain is presented as the substitute for multiplicity.

## 3. Decoding the new argument

Format for each piece: what the note says, relevant background, my reading of its role, confidence in that reading.

### 3.1 The headline idea: a chain of theta lifts stands in for multiplicity

- **[NOTE]** (p.1, items 8, 1, 4) The argument is "a descendant of Stepanov's". Fourier-analytic ideas from a toy setting led them to "chaining theta lifts" in a "natively automorphic manner". Each lift is described as "mimicking multiplicity". For a von Mangoldt sum of length X, Claude suggested a chain of length √X. Item 8: iterated theta lifts U(X₀) → U(X₁) → ... → U(X_m) between totally definite unitary groups, with π ↦ π ⊞ μ_j[t], "wins multiplicity m".
- **[BG]** A theta lift transfers automorphic forms from one group of a "dual pair" to the other, using a theta function as an integral kernel. On the level of parameters, a lift typically adds a summand: π ⊞ χ[t] is standard Arthur-parameter notation for "π plus a character χ times a t-dimensional piece" (Adams' conjecture describes theta lifts this way). A totally definite unitary group is compact at every real place, so automorphic forms on it are functions on a finite set. That makes everything algebraic and integral, which is why the note talks about divisibility, masses and covolumes.
- **[BG]** In Stepanov's proof the multiplicity is taken around √q when counting about q points. So √X for a sum of length X is the number the analogy predicts. This fits the author's "what else could it be?"
- **[READ]** Each lift step adds one more L-function factor or one more vanishing condition to a period, the way one more derivative adds one more order of vanishing. A chain of m lifts yields an object that "vanishes to order m" in the right arithmetic sense. Then a counting or size inequality (see 3.9, 3.10) plays the role of "degree ≥ zeros × multiplicity".
- **Confidence:** High that this is the central idea as the note presents it. Low on the precise mechanism. "Each lift is one derivative" is the note's analogy, not a stated theorem.

### 3.2 The toy setting: the "qN = M^k + R" sign-only proof of Weil and Deligne

- **[NOTE]** (p.1, p.2, item 3, fn 3) The author had a new proof, in the function-field "toy setting", of Weil's and Deligne's theorems that uses only signs. fn 3 gives the sum

  > Σ over h, x₁, ..., x_k of t(x₁)···t(x_k) · e_q( h·(x₁x₂ + ... + x_{k−1}x_k) ).

  Summing over h first gives the left-hand side. Bertini gives it a definite sign from middle cohomology, "one off" the sign on M^k. M^k is the "zero-mode" on the right. R is of square-root quality by "iterating Parseval k times". Opposite signs mean "no cancellation of inverse roots, QED". The quadric is called the "path" quadric, a simplification of a "complete graph" structure coming from averaging over a family of characters; Gauss sum cancellation is replaced by iterated Parseval.
- **[READ] Reconstruction (I'm fairly confident of the skeleton):**
  - Let t be a function on F_q whose sum M = Σ t(x) you want to bound (for example a character sum, or "points in the fibre minus the expected number").
  - Summing e_q(h·Q) over all h gives q if Q = 0 and 0 otherwise. So the full sum equals q·N, where N is the t-weighted count of points on the quadric Q = x₁x₂ + x₂x₃ + ... + x_{k−1}x_k = 0. ("Path": the variables are linked in a chain. Whether Q is the path x₁x₂ + x₂x₃ + ... or the pairing x₁x₂ + x₃x₄ + ... is not determinable from the formula alone; the word "path" suggests the former.)
  - The h = 0 term is (Σ t)^k = M^k.
  - For each h ≠ 0, the sum is a chain: multiply by t, Fourier transform, multiply by t, Fourier transform, ... Each Fourier transform preserves L² norm (Parseval), so the whole term is bounded with square-root savings at each of the k steps. That's R.
  - So qN = M^k + R with R small. Now the sign: N is a point count on a hypersurface section, which (Bertini plus Lefschetz) only has interesting cohomology in the middle degree, so its sign is known. If that sign is opposite to the sign of M^k, the two cannot cancel each other, so each is at most |R|. Then |M| ≤ |R|^(1/k), and letting k grow gives the square-root bound up to a factor q^(O(1/k)), the "inch".
- **[BG]** Using high powers plus a known sign to rule out cancellation is in the spirit of Rankin's trick in Deligne's proof. Presenting it with a quadric and iterated Parseval is, as far as I know, new and unpublished. **So this toy proof is itself an unverified claim.**
- **Role [READ]:** It shows square-root cancellation can be proved from two inputs only: (a) a positivity or sign statement and (b) repeated Fourier duality. Both have analogues over number fields (automorphy and theta correspondence), whereas "geometry of C × C" does not.
- **Confidence:** Medium-high on the skeleton. Low on the details of where the sign comes from and what "vectorize to go up dimensions" means.

### 3.3 The "natively automorphic determinant" and multiplicity 2 on O(2,n)

- **[NOTE]** (p.3) The author had been searching for a "natively-automorphic" determinant by analogy with the definite sign of the left side of qN = M^k + R "meaning holo/automorphy over a function field". Once they "saw multiplicity 2 on O(2,n)" via this determinant, with more multiplicity from more theta lifts, "we were going to win". Items 1 and 4: "Stepanov determinant"; W(1_S) ≥ 0 via a determinant, read as "Stepanov with multiplicity one".
- **[BG]** Over a function field, the sign of a cohomological contribution decides whether an L-function is a polynomial (holomorphic) or has poles. Over number fields, holomorphy of L-functions comes from automorphy. O(2,n) pairs with SL₂ in the theta correspondence and is the setting of Borcherds' lifts. Stepanov's auxiliary polynomial exists because a linear system has a nonzero solution, which can be rephrased as a determinant condition (as in interpolation determinants in transcendence theory).
- **[READ]** The function-field proof's "sign from cohomology" gets replaced by "positivity or holomorphy from automorphy", expressed as a determinant (Gram-type) built from automorphic objects. Multiplicity one reproduces only known-strength statements. The turning point was an automorphic construction that produced order 2, showing the multiplicity-one barrier could be broken, after which iteration was the obvious move.
- **Confidence:** Medium on the role as the turning point (the note says so directly). Low on what the determinant is.

### 3.4 The quantity being bounded and the positivity framework

- **[NOTE]** Item 5: they "definitively switch to Λ", namely

  > Σ_{n≤X} Λ(n)·(X/n − 1)₊ − X log X + (1 + γ)·X.

  "Guinand-Weil had the sign." A "variance test function trick" makes things "trace-formula-like". He asks how to "pigeonhole / Turán" over n, X, or it. Mention of a "Mehta-like integral over twists or at infinity". Item 4: W(1_S) ≥ 0. Item 6: W(1_(0,1] + (∼X^η)·1_(0,1/X]) ≥ 0 "gives > 1 − η".
- **[BG, checked]** The displayed expression is a smoothed prime-counting error term. The Mellin transform of (X/n − 1)₊ is X^s/(s(s−1)); the double pole at s = 1 produces exactly X log X − (1 + γ)X; what remains is −Σ_ρ X^ρ/(ρ(ρ−1)) plus bounded terms. That sum over zeros converges absolutely, so this is a well-behaved test quantity. RH is equivalent to it being O(X^(1/2+ε)) (in fact O(√X) under RH). So "a von Mangoldt sum of length X" on page 1 is this.
- **[READ]** The target is square-root cancellation in this smoothed sum, attacked via Weil positivity on indicator-type test functions. Item 6 looks like an early proof-of-concept: positivity for one specific two-step test function yields a partial result with parameter η. The natural guess is a zero-free region or an error exponent tied to 1 − η, improving as η grows. I can't pin down which.
- **Confidence:** High on what the sum is. Medium that Weil positivity is the frame. Low on what "> 1 − η" quantifies.

### 3.5 Periods: GGP, seesaw, Rallis

- **[NOTE]** Item 7: determinants for W(·) contain "the desired periods, indeed GGP periods, with values 0, 1 meaning interlacing weights, or up to m non-interlacing ones". Item 12: by a seesaw, the period of the iterated lift is "a Bessel period independent of the Hecke characters, Rallis".
- **[BG]** Gan-Gross-Prasad: for U(n) ⊃ U(n−1), the space of invariant functionals on π ⊗ σ has dimension 0 or 1, and nonvanishing of the period is tied to a central L-value. For compact groups this is classical branching: a representation of U(n−1) occurs in one of U(n) (with multiplicity 1) exactly when the highest weights interlace. Bessel periods are one of the two families of GGP periods (the other is Fourier-Jacobi). A seesaw identity (Kudla) swaps a period of a theta lift for a period on the other side of the dual pair. The Rallis inner product formula expresses the norm of a theta lift as an L-value times local factors.
- **[READ]** The entries of the positivity determinants are period integrals. Their being 0 or 1 dimensional gives rigid combinatorial control (interlacing), and after m lifts up to m interlacing conditions can fail, which matches "multiplicity m". The seesaw then shows the key period doesn't depend on the auxiliary Hecke characters μ_j used along the chain, so those characters are free parameters to average over.
- **Confidence:** Medium. The background match (0/1 and interlacing) is solid. The architecture is a guess.

### 3.6 Arithmetic size: Chowla-Selberg, Weyl, Siegel mass, divisibility

- **[NOTE]** Item 2: D^p ≡ 0 on a CM curve for p inert in K (D the invariant derivation), and D^a θ(0)/Ω^a is divisible by p^(a/p + a/p³ + ... − O(log a)). Item 9: norms of the lifts, level, and "cancellation of order-m terms with proven divisibility via Chowla-Selberg, Weyl, and Siegel mass". Item 10: sufficient lower bound on divisibility of the m-fold lift. Item 18: covolumes, theta series' index, congruences between lifts and forms.
- **[BG]** On an elliptic curve in characteristic p, the p-th iterate of the invariant derivation is (Hasse invariant)·D, so it vanishes when the curve is supersingular, which is the case for a CM curve at an inert prime. Chowla-Selberg expresses the period Ω of a CM elliptic curve via Gamma values. The Siegel mass formula counts lattices in a genus weighted by automorphisms, and governs volumes for definite groups. "Weyl" is probably the Weyl dimension formula (my guess).
- **[READ]** This is where "differentiating whole numbers" gets a concrete meaning. Derivatives of theta functions at CM points, divided by the right power of the period, are algebraic integers, and at inert primes they are divisible by growing powers of p. **High p-adic divisibility is the integer-world stand-in for vanishing to high order.** The m-fold lift needs a proven lower bound on such divisibility (item 10) and an upper bound on its size or norm (item 9). An integer that is highly divisible but small must be zero, or an integer that is nonzero must be at least as large as its divisors. That is the arithmetic version of "degree ≥ zeros × multiplicity".
- **Confidence:** Medium. This reading makes the pieces cohere, and item 2's dates (Oct 9, right after "Stepanov determinant") support it, but the note never states it.

### 3.7 Bost slope inequality

- **[NOTE]** Item 13, dated 10/6, no elaboration.
- **[BG]** Bost's slope method is the Arakelov-geometry packaging of the auxiliary-function method from transcendence theory. For an injective evaluation map between Hermitian vector bundles over a number ring, it bounds the arithmetic degree of the source by slopes of the target plus the heights of the maps. It replaces "Siegel's lemma, construct a polynomial, count zeros" with one inequality.
- **[READ]** This is the number-field replacement for Stepanov's degree count: the bookkeeping device that turns "many vanishing conditions with multiplicity" plus "size bounds" into a contradiction or an inequality.
- **Confidence:** Medium-high on the role, since that's what the slope inequality is for.

### 3.8 Iwasawa tower

- **[NOTE]** Item 11: Iwasawa tower "via analogy to pigeonhole on F_{q^n}/F_q". Item 19: "precise choice of the Iwasawa tower, specifically using Behrend!!" fn 7: this trick is "obviously going to be unnecessary".
- **[BG]** Iwasawa's founding analogy: the cyclotomic Z_ℓ-tower of a number field plays the role of the constant-field extensions F_{q^n} of a function field. Weil's proof needs all the F_{q^n} at once (section 2.1).
- **[READ]** The argument proves a bound at each level of a tower of number fields and then passes up the tower to remove constants or cancellation, as the F_{q^n} trick does.
- **Behrend:** only a surname is given. Felix Behrend's 1946 construction of large sets with no three-term arithmetic progressions is one candidate (the task brief assumes it). Kai Behrend's trace formula for stacks is another. **I can't tell which from the note.** Either way the author expects to remove it.
- **Confidence:** High on the analogy. Unknown on Behrend.

### 3.9 The cost computation, "2/3", and "division"

- **[NOTE]** Item 14: precise computation of the "cost" of each lift shows "one would stop at 2/3 a priori"; attempts to get a main term and "transfer constant to a function field or kill via twisting"; "need for 'division'". Item 15: glue an integral structure across Hecke characters "for a 'gcd' i.e. extra averaging".
- **[READ]** Each lift buys one order of multiplicity but costs something (level, norm growth). Balancing gain against cost naively gives an exponent of 2/3 where 1/2 is needed, probably meaning a bound of quality X^(2/3) or zeros excluded only for Re s > 2/3. To get past it you divide out a large common factor ("gcd") shared across the family of lifts indexed by Hecke characters, which needs a common integral structure. "Division" may also echo Stepanov's final step of dividing degree by multiplicity.
- **Confidence:** Low to medium. "2/3" being an exponent short of 1/2 is the natural reading but the note doesn't say what the 2/3 measures.

### 3.10 Relative trace formula and the sixfold cover of GL₅

- **[NOTE]** Item 3: "automorphy" of N wanted "since μ is automorphic from θ on sixfold cover of GL₅ as a period integral of thetas"; the quadric has an interpretation on a CM curve such as C/Z[ω] "as Weil"; "put Poincaré bundle on the edges to get θs". Item 16: relative trace formula, "seen in a different half-plane argument" via the same automorphy of μ, reduced by Cauchy-Schwarz to bounding the norm of a projected Poincaré series; approximate orthogonality of the columns of the integral structure. fn 3: a normalized Gauss sum of order #μ_K lives via θ on the #μ_K-fold cover of GL₂.
- **[BG]** Gauss sums of order n appear in the Fourier coefficients of Eisenstein series on n-fold metaplectic covers of GL₂ (Kubota). Patterson proved that the cubic theta function on the threefold cover has cubic Gauss sums as coefficients. Beyond the cubic case the coefficients of theta functions on covers of GL₂ are only partly understood, and the sextic case is conjectural as far as I know. So fn 3's statement about a sixfold cover should be treated as the note's own claim, not as background. Q(ω) has six roots of unity, hence "sixfold". The relative trace formula (Jacquet) compares period integrals instead of characters. Sections of the Poincaré bundle on E × E are theta functions.
- **[READ]** The number-field analogue of the path quadric attaches a theta function to each edge of the path, living on a CM elliptic curve. The whole count N becomes a period integral of thetas, hence automorphic, which supplies the "sign". The relative trace formula is the analytic engine for bounding the remainder, in the role of iterated Parseval.
- **Which μ?** In items 3 and 16 "μ" is not defined. It could be the Möbius function or something else. In item 8, μ_j[t] is most likely a Hecke character (item 12 speaks of "the Hecke characters"). μ_K in fn 3 is the roots of unity of K. Don't identify these with each other.
- **Confidence:** Low. This is the least decodable part of the note.

### 3.11 Leftover cohomology classes and a weakening of Vandiver

- **[NOTE]** Item 17: for classes in H¹(o_E[1/ℓ], Q_ℓ/Z_ℓ(−e)) that "we couldn't kill by twisting, fight for a significant weakening of Vandiver". fn 6: still "pretty strong"; he believes vanishing of a number of K-groups of Z follows, and "cyclicity up to 100ish maybe"; mentions Lichtenbaum.
- **[BG]** Vandiver's conjecture: ℓ does not divide the class number of the real subfield of Q(ζ_ℓ). It is open. Kurihara showed it is equivalent to K_{4n}(Z) = 0 for all n. Galois cohomology groups of this shape are exactly where such class-group obstructions live, and Lichtenbaum's conjectures tie K-groups to zeta values.
- **[READ]** Some obstruction terms in the integral or divisibility argument are Galois cohomology classes. Most are removed by twisting with characters. The remainder needs an actual theorem about class groups in the tower, a partial Vandiver. **If so, the proof contains a second major new result as a lemma**, which is another thing reviewers will scrutinise.
- **Confidence:** Medium on the role. The footnote's consequences are the author's stated beliefs, hedged by him.

### 3.12 How it might fit together [READ, whole paragraph]

Target: square-root cancellation in a smoothed von Mangoldt sum of length X (3.4), framed as a positivity statement. Template: the toy proof (3.2), where a sign plus iterated Fourier duality gives square-root cancellation. Translation: sign becomes automorphy (3.3); Fourier duality becomes theta correspondence; one step of the path becomes one theta lift between definite unitary groups (3.1); multiplicity becomes p-adic divisibility of integral period values (3.6); the degree count becomes a slope inequality (3.7); extension fields become an Iwasawa tower (3.8). Obstacles: lift costs cap the naive method at 2/3 (3.9), fixed by extra averaging and division; residual Galois cohomology classes (3.11), fixed by a partial Vandiver. Analytic control of remainders: relative trace formula (3.10). Take m ≈ √X.

I'd put this at "plausible outline consistent with every fragment", not more.

## 4. The major unblockers, ranked

Ranking is by how much weight the note itself puts on each.

1. **Chained theta lifts as a substitute for multiplicity, with chain length √X.** (p.1 "the line"; item 8.)
   Plain English: you can't differentiate integers, but you can repeat a symmetry-transfer operation, and each repetition does the job of one derivative.
   Animation: a ladder between stacked platforms labelled U(X₀), U(X₁), ...; each rung flattens a curve's contact with the axis by one more order; a counter climbs toward √X.
   Attribution per note: chain length suggested by Claude.

### Draft narration (about 730 words)

**1.** On November 20th, 2026, the number theorist Levent Alpöge posted a four-page note. In it he says that he and Claude, an AI model, have found a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis, and that the proof has been checked line by line in the Lean proof assistant. It's a claim, not yet a confirmed result. Here's what it says.

**2.** Primes thin out as numbers grow, but in a regular way. Count the primes up to x and you get a staircase. A smooth curve, the logarithmic integral, tracks it closely. The question is how closely. The Riemann Hypothesis says the gap is about as small as it could be: roughly the square root of x, times a logarithm. Up to a trillion there are about 37.6 billion primes, and the curve misses by only about 38 thousand. The hypothesis says the fit stays that tight forever.

**3.** Riemann tied this to one function, called zeta. It's built from the whole numbers, yet it encodes the primes exactly. Extended to the complex plane, zeta has infinitely many zeros inside a vertical strip. Riemann's guess, from 1859, is that every one of them sits on the centre line, real part one half. Trillions have been checked by computer. All are on the line. Nobody has proved they all are.

**4.** Each zero contributes a wave. Add up the waves and the prime staircase appears. A zero's height sets the wave's pitch. Its distance from the centre line sets how loud it gets. If every zero is on the line, no wave is louder than the rest, and the error stays near the square root of x. The Generalized Riemann Hypothesis says the same about a family of related functions, which govern primes in arithmetic patterns.

**5.** There's a parallel world where this is already a theorem. Replace whole numbers with polynomials over a finite field, and the question becomes one about counting points on a curve. André Weil proved that version in the 1940s. Pierre Deligne extended it in 1974. And in 1969, Sergei Stepanov found an elementary proof for certain curves, which Enrico Bombieri and Wolfgang Schmidt later extended.

**6.** Stepanov's trick: build an auxiliary polynomial that vanishes at every point you want to count. And it doesn't just vanish. It touches zero to high order at each point, flattening against the axis. A polynomial of limited degree can only have so many zeros, counted with multiplicity. So high multiplicity forces a tight count. And multiplicity comes from derivatives.

**7.** That's where the integers resist. You can differentiate a polynomial. You can't differentiate the number seven. Mathematicians have long hoped for a hidden geometry beneath the integers where that makes sense. Alpöge writes that multiplicity over the integers is hard to find for this reason.

**8.** His note describes a substitute. In the theory of automorphic forms there's an operation called a theta lift, which carries a highly symmetric object from one group to another. According to the note, each lift mimics one order of multiplicity. Chain the lifts into a ladder, and each rung buys one more. To control a sum over primes up to X, Claude suggested a ladder with about the square root of X rungs. Alpöge calls this the line that will make mathematicians fall out of their chairs.

**9.** Around that ladder sits a lot of machinery: a positivity test for the hypothesis due to Weil, formulas that measure the size of each lift, and towers of number fields standing in for Weil's growing finite fields. The note lists twenty steps, and says some of its descriptions may need correcting.

**10.** The timeline runs from October 6th to November 16th. Alpöge says he supplied the guiding analogy and a new proof in the finite-field setting. He says Claude then outpaced him on the technical side, in an area where he isn't an expert. A written-out version runs past 250,000 pages. He chose not to post that, and released only the Lean code.

**11.** So what happens now? Others need to compile that code independently. They need to confirm that the theorem it states really is the Riemann Hypothesis, and that no extra assumptions slipped in. And mathematicians will want a proof they can read and understand. Alpöge himself expects the argument to shrink a great deal. Until those checks are done, this is a serious claim from a serious mathematician. It isn't yet a theorem.

- **2.** The figures are observed, not conditional: π(10^12) = 37,607,912,018 and li(10^12) − π(10^12) = 38,263. - **3.** "Built from the whole numbers" refers to Σ n^(−s), valid only for Re s > 1; the zeros live where that series doesn't converge, hence "extended". "Trillions" is accurate (over 10^13). "Infinitely many zeros inside a strip" omits trivial zeros; fine.
